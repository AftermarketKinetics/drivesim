How it works
  1. Export the whole robot as a STEP assembly from your CAD (not one part), with part names kept. Fasteners and electronics can stay in.
  2. Upload it below. A big assembly takes a minute or two to import.
  3. Answer Claude's questions. From the CAD alone it can't tell which parts are wheels and weapon, which way the weapon spins, what things are made of, or which motors and gear ratios you use. It asks in one message; answer what you know, and it writes and checks the robot file.
  4. When the page says "Ready to drive", click "Drive it". The sim runs in Chrome or Edge (it needs WebGPU). Your robot starts facing an orange opponent. The buttons at the top right pick the mode, and each switch restarts the round:
    • Copy (the default): an orange copy of your robot that sits still, armed but with its sticks centred. Good for practising your driving and lining up hits.
    • Box: an orange box as heavy as your robot, to push around and hit.
    • AI: the AI drives the copy. After a 3-2-1 countdown it comes at you with its weapon at full, for a 3-minute bout. At 0:00 both robots are disarmed, "TIME" shows, and Rematch starts again.
    • AI vs AI: an AI drives your robot too, so you can watch it fight with no radio set up. Use it to see how a robot handles, hits and takes hits before you drive it.
    In AI and AI vs AI, the dropdown next to the buttons picks the opponent: your own copy or any public robot, listed by weight class (the house simple_bot comes in 1, 3, 12 and 30 lb). Hard hits leave glowing marks where they land; untick "Marks" (top right) to hide them. The "Physics" slider beside it sets 2–4 kHz; light robots with fast weapons need 4 (see "drivesim physics/compensations" below).
  5. Share your robot as an opponent (optional). On your robot's page, tick "Publicly accessible" and choose its weight class. Everyone can then pick it for their AI to drive. They get its 3D model and robot file, never your conversation with Claude. Untick it to stop sharing.
  6. Plug in your radio (below) and move a stick so the browser sees it. Arm with SA. AI vs AI needs no radio.

The top-left readout shows the radio, arming, speed, weapon rpm, tilt and "physics N× real time": below 1× the sim can't keep up and runs slow.

Set up your radio (EdgeTX + ELRS)

Download accelerate-elrs-pocket.yml: a model for the RadioMaster Pocket on EdgeTX 2.10. The same sticks and switches drive the sim and a real ELRS robot, so what you practise is what you drive.

ChannelControlDoes
CH1right stick left/rightturn
CH2right stick up/downdrive
CH3left stick up/downweapon (stick down = off)
CH5SAarm (down = armed)

CH4 (left stick left/right) and CH6–8 (SD, SE, SB) are spare: they do nothing in the sim, and are there for robots that use them.

  1. Copy the model onto the radio. Plug the radio in by USB and choose USB Storage (or put its SD card in a reader). Open MODELS/ and copy the file in as modelNN.yml, using a number that isn't there yet: copying onto an existing number replaces that model.
  2. Select it: eject, press MDL and pick Accelerate. Zero any trims. On load, the radio wants SA up and the weapon stick down.
  3. For the sim: plug in by USB and choose USB Joystick (HID). Leave the joystick mode on Classic, the default. Open the drive page and move a stick; the readout changes from "NO RADIO" to "radio connected".
  4. For a real robot: the Pocket's internal ELRS module sends the same channels. Bind your receiver as usual; ELRS arms from CH5.

Another EdgeTX radio works too: give it a model with the four channels above (CH1 aileron, CH2 elevator, CH3 throttle, CH5 a two-position switch), no expo or rates, and Classic USB joystick mode.

drivesim physics/compensations

What the simulation does, and where it corrects the physics engine (rapier3d 0.36) so a robot behaves like the real one. The full reasoning is in docs/drivesim.md §6.

Stepping

  • Physics at 2, 3 or 4 kHz, picked with the Physics slider at the top right of the drive page (2 by default, remembered per browser). The radio, the AI and the motors' duties update at 1 kHz whatever the rate.
  • The rate is set by the fastest weapon, not the controls: a 10,000 rpm weapon on a 1 ms step throws the robot. Small, heavy, fast weapons on light robots (a beetle's 45 mm bar at 10,600 rpm) need 4 kHz to stay physical in hits. At 2 kHz they can fling robots unrealistically. 4 kHz costs about 1.7× the CPU; watch "physics N× real time".
  • Contacts as exact as the engine offers: one friction cone per contact point, fresh contact points every step (so spinning wheels don't reuse stale ones), stiff critically damped contacts, 4 solver iterations.

Robot bodies

  • Chassis: one rigid body with the exact mass, centre of mass and inertia of every part in your CAD. It collides through the convex hulls of its 12 biggest parts; small parts (fasteners, magnets, electronics) keep their mass but don't collide.
  • Wheels: a body each on a hinge, colliding as a cylinder at the measured radius and width. Grip is the tyre's μ from the robot file on a μ = 1 floor; the chassis and weapon slide at μ 0.3.
  • Weapon: a body on a hinge at its true rpm, with gyroscopic precession and continuous collision detection on. Only the parts reaching at least half its radius collide; spindles and pulleys inside the robot keep their mass.
  • Nothing bounces (restitution 0); energy is lost in hits instead (below).

Motors and gearboxes

  • DC motor model per wheel and weapon: current from duty, battery voltage, back-EMF and resistance, clamped to the ESC's current limit, times kv, gear ratio and efficiency.
  • Applied as a torque on the rotor and an equal, opposite one on the chassis, so the reaction torque is real: spin-up tips the robot, and drive torque pitches it.
  • Compensation: reflected motor inertia. Each wheel and weapon carries its motor's rotor inertia × ratio², as a real gearmotor's output does. On a 27:1 beetle drive that is most of what the wheel feels when accelerating. It also keeps the motor model stable at the physics step.
  • Compensation: no gyroscopic term on wheels, because that lumped inertia would overstate their spin's angular momentum about ratio times.
  • Compensation: gearbox coupling. The lump would push ratio times too much spin-up reaction into the chassis, enough to flip a robot on every throttle slam. Each step adds the torques that turn it back into a real gearbox (worked out from the gearbox's true energy).
  • Compensation: smoothed coupling. Those torques use the previous step's accelerations, which on a light robot with high-ratio gearmotors can feed on themselves (a 1 kg beetle on 27:1 once blew itself across the arena unpowered). They are low-pass filtered per robot, at a strength set by how big the gearboxes are against the chassis. That lands on the exact answer in one step for any robot.

Hits

  • Compensation: impact loss. A rigid engine hands a struck robot all of a weapon tooth's speed. Real hits lose energy into deformation. After any step where a robot's non-floor contacts exceed 5 g, it keeps half the velocity change that step gave it. All its parts are scaled together, and the weapon still loses all the spin the hit took.
  • Impact marks: a contact that would change a robot's speed by 2 m/s or more in one step glows where it landed, orange to red by strength ("Marks" hides them).

Arena and opponents

  • NHRL's 16 ft cage: dead-flat painted plywood floor, polycarbonate walls on a timber skirt, and a roof so a hit can't throw a robot out.
  • Opponents: a braked copy, a box as heavy as your robot, or an AI driving the copy or any public robot with its own masses, motors and gearing. The AI turns with damped steering, waits for its weapon (until 90% speed, backing off if close) and reverses when stuck.

Known limits

  • At 2 kHz, a small heavy weapon can still make energy in a hit; use 4 kHz for beetles.
  • A bar or disc sitting a couple of millimetres off the floor touches down when the nose dips under hard braking or reversing, and can flip the robot. That is likely what the real robot would do too.
  • Turning hard with a vertical weapon at speed can roll the robot over through gyroscopic torque. That is real physics.
  • Parts don't break or deform.
